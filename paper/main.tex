\begin{abstract}
Knowledge graphs used by agentic systems are often treated as flat stores of extracted triples, with little record of who owns a fact, why it was admitted, or how it should be used downstream. We argue that reliable agentic knowledge systems require governance as a first-class graph layer. We propose \system{}, a principled multi-agent framework for constructing Governed Knowledge Graphs. A domain classifier first induces entity and relation types directly from document content, allowing the system to handle arbitrary domains without predefined ontologies. Candidate triples are then assigned to domain owners, reviewed against supporting evidence, admitted through explicit governance decisions, and stored with audit metadata. As new documents arrive, the same governance protocol routes candidate updates to domain owners for review before admission, allowing the graph to grow and reorganize without bulk re-extraction. This ownership layer is reused during question answering: queries are routed to domain-specific graph experts rather than answered through undifferentiated retrieval. Empirically, \system{} improves strict triple F1 by 49\% and fuzzy/mapped triple F1 by 63\% on SciERC, and improves MuSiQue QA over official GraphRAG by 13.8 exact-match points and 9.3 token-F1 points.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge graphs are increasingly used as memory substrates for agentic systems, but most current pipelines still treat them as flat stores of extracted triples. In the standard pattern, one or more agents extract candidate facts, a controller merges them into a single graph, and the pipeline terminates. This design is simple, but it leaves several practical questions unanswered: who owns a fact, what evidence justified its inclusion, how should conflicting updates be resolved, and how should downstream applications decide which part of the graph to consult? A flat graph can store relations, but it does not by itself provide the organizational structure needed for controlled updates, auditability, or domain-aware reasoning.

These limitations become more visible when knowledge graphs are used by active systems rather than passive analytics pipelines. Agentic systems must continually ingest new information, decide whether candidate facts should be admitted, revise facts when better evidence appears, and route queries to the right portion of the graph. In this setting, a graph is not merely a repository of extracted relations. It is a memory structure that must support ownership, evidence-grounded review, incremental update, and downstream use. We argue that governance is therefore not an optional add-on, but a necessary graph layer for reliable agentic knowledge systems.

We propose Multi-Agent Governance Graph (\system{}), a principled framework for constructing Governed Knowledge Graphs. In \system{}, candidate facts are not inserted directly into memory. A domain classifier first induces a working schema from document content, allowing the system to operate without predefined ontologies in open-world settings. Candidate entities and relations are then extracted, linked to evidence, assigned to domain owners, and routed through an explicit governance protocol before admission. Each admitted triple is stored with ownership and audit metadata, and the same ownership structure is reused when the graph is queried downstream. As new documents arrive, the graph can be incrementally enriched through the same governed update process rather than through bulk re-extraction and blind merge.

\system{} is organized into three coordinated layers. The \emph{creation layer} processes documents through a multi-stage extraction pipeline with blackboard-based deliberation for uncertain entities, relations, and triples. The \emph{governance layer} routes candidate triples to domain owners, reviews them against evidence and local domain memory, and records explicit admission decisions. The \emph{application layer} builds downstream functionality on top of the governed graph, including domain-routed question answering and incremental self-organization as new documents are processed. The central architectural idea is that the same domain structure that governs what enters memory also determines how memory is later accessed and updated.

We evaluate \system{} in both fixed-schema and open-world settings. On SciERC, governed construction improves admitted graph quality over a flat extraction baseline while preserving entity quality. On MuSiQue, the resulting domain-routed QA system outperforms official GraphRAG on graph-based question answering. These results support the broader claim of the paper: knowledge graphs for agentic systems should not be treated as flat collections of extracted triples, but as governed memory structures in which every fact has an owner, evidence, an admission path, and an audit trail.

The contributions of this work are threefold. First, we introduce the Governed Knowledge Graph, a representation that augments entities and triples with domain ownership and explicit governance decisions. Second, we present a multi-agent construction pipeline in which governance is integrated into graph creation rather than applied post hoc. Third, we show empirically that governed construction improves extraction quality and that the resulting domain-owned graph structure is useful for downstream graph-based question answering.

\section{Governed Knowledge Graph}

\textbf{Definition 1.}
A Governed Knowledge Graph is a tuple
\[
G = (E, T, D, \phi, \gamma),
\]
where $E$ is a set of entities, $T \subseteq E \times R \times E$ is a set of typed triples, $D = \{d_1, \ldots, d_k\}$ is a set of domains, $\phi: E \to 2^D$ is an ownership function assigning each entity to one or more governing domains, and $\gamma: T \to \{\texttt{approve}, \texttt{reject}, \texttt{revise}, \texttt{escalate}, \texttt{auto\_approve}\}$ is a governance function over proposed triples.

This definition differs from a standard knowledge graph in two ways. First, the graph contains an explicit organizational layer through domains and ownership. Second, admission into the triple set is mediated through governance rather than direct insertion. In implementation, this is realized through a two-phase protocol: propose a triple, obtain a governance decision, and then commit that decision. This changes the graph from a passive fact store into a managed memory structure.

The ownership function is written as $\phi: E \to 2^D$, rather than $\phi: E \to D$, because one entity may legitimately belong to more than one domain. In a scientific corpus, the same entity can participate in multiple conceptual regions of the graph: a method may belong to a methods domain and also sit inside a task-specific domain; a biomarker may belong to both an inflammation domain and a cardiometabolic domain. Using $2^D$ means $\phi(e)$ returns a set of owners, not a single owner. This is what makes cross-domain governance possible. Once entities have domain assignments, a triple can be classified as single-owner, cross-domain, or unowned depending on the ownership of its endpoints.

Operationally, the data structure consists of more than entities and triples. A governed triple also carries an ownership assignment, a governance action, a rationale, and an audit record. In the implementation, every admitted triple passes through \texttt{propose\_triple(...)} , produces a \texttt{GovernanceDecision}, and is then committed through \texttt{commit\_decision(...)}. This is the concrete mechanism that instantiates $\gamma$. The graph therefore stores not only what is known, but also how that knowledge entered memory and who was responsible for it.

\section{System Overview}

The system has three layers. The first is the creation layer, which turns documents into entities and triples. The second is the governance layer, which organizes knowledge into domains and controls admission into memory. The third is the application layer, which uses the governed graph for downstream tasks such as question answering and incremental enrichment.

\section{Knowledge Graph Creation Workflow}

The system receives a corpus of documents. A top-level orchestrator agent manages the end-to-end creation process over the full corpus and over each document instance. The pipeline is mostly sequential. Each specialized agent transforms or validates the current representation and passes it to the next stage. The system only activates deliberation when an agent signals uncertainty or conflict.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Governed KG Creation}
\label{alg:gkg-creation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Corpus $\mathcal{C}$, Governed KG $G=(E,T,D,\phi,\gamma)$
\FOR{each document $x \in \mathcal{C}$}
    \STATE $\text{segments} \leftarrow \textsc{DocumentProcessor}(x)$
    \STATE $\text{schema} \leftarrow \textsc{DomainClassifier}(\text{segments})$
    \STATE $D_{\text{boot}} \leftarrow \textsc{BootstrapDomains}(\text{schema})$
    \STATE $\text{entities} \leftarrow \textsc{EntityExtractor}(\text{segments}, \text{schema})$
    \STATE $\phi \leftarrow \textsc{AssignEntitiesToDomains}(\text{entities}, D_{\text{boot}})$
    \STATE $\text{triples} \leftarrow \textsc{RelationExtractor}(\text{segments}, \text{entities}, \text{schema})$
    \STATE $\text{linked} \leftarrow \textsc{EvidenceLinker}(\text{triples}, \text{segments})$
    \STATE $\text{verified} \leftarrow \textsc{VerificationAgent}(\text{entities}, \text{linked}, x)$
    \FOR{each triple $t$ in $\text{verified}$}
        \STATE $\delta \leftarrow \textsc{propose\_triple}(t)$
        \STATE $\textsc{commit\_decision}(\delta)$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Governed KG $G$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The workflow is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item The \texttt{DocumentProcessor} segments each document into coherent chunks.
\item The \texttt{DomainClassifier} derives a working schema for the document. In benchmark mode it uses a fixed schema; in open-world mode it discovers entity and relation types from the content. From this schema, the system bootstraps preliminary domains before any triples are admitted. This is the first point at which governance becomes active during creation.
\item The \texttt{EntityExtractor} identifies entity mentions, canonical labels, and candidate types. The shortest-meaningful-noun-phrase rule is used only to normalize the entity span itself, so that entities are anchored to compact canonical labels rather than overly long surface forms. Relation extraction is not limited to noun pairs: downstream agents still use the full sentence context, including predicates, modifiers, and supporting evidence, to determine whether a typed relation should be proposed between the extracted entities. The extractor is also instructed to preserve short scientific terms, abbreviations, tool names, and task phrases rather than discarding them as generic text.
\item Once entities exist, they are assigned to one or more domains. This partially instantiates the ownership function ($\phi$), allowing the system to know who owns a proposed fact during creation.
\item The \texttt{RelationExtractor} identifies relations between current entities and produces candidate triples. In fixed-schema benchmark mode, the extractor is constrained to the benchmark label set; in open-world mode, it can propose new relation structure.
\item The \texttt{EvidenceLinker} grounds each candidate triple in the source document by retrieving explicit supporting text spans and source references. It is instructed to quote or localize evidence directly from the document rather than generate free-form justifications, and these grounded spans are then inspected by the verification and governance stages before the triple can be admitted.
\item The \texttt{VerificationAgent} removes unsupported or low-quality extractions and outputs a verified set of triples suitable for admission review.
\item At that point, the \texttt{KnowledgeOrganizer} and governance layer take over. Each verified triple is proposed to the governed graph rather than inserted directly. The system routes the triple to the relevant domain or domains, produces a governance decision, commits the decision, and appends that decision to the audit log.
\end{enumerate}

There are several important operational details that the workflow relies on. The corpus is processed incrementally, so later documents see a richer memory than earlier documents. Entity resolution happens against both the current document and the pre-existing graph, allowing later documents to attach to canonical entities created earlier in the corpus. Domain bootstrapping begins before triple admission, but ownership remains provisional as the graph grows. Early domains function as lightweight organizational shells rather than fixed ontologies. New facts are proposed from the source document first and only then evaluated against the current graph, so existing triples do not deterministically force new information into pre-existing relation patterns. Prior domain structure is used as review context rather than as a hard constraint. Governance happens after extraction-time deliberation, not instead of it: deliberation asks whether an uncertain extraction should survive, while governance asks how a surviving triple should enter memory.

\section{Deliberation, Blackboard, and Voting}

The pipeline is sequential by default. Deliberation is an exceptional path triggered when an agent reports low confidence. In the current implementation, a threshold around 0.7 is used as the key decision boundary. If an agent's confidence is above threshold, the pipeline continues. If confidence falls below threshold, the agent raises a hypothesis.

A hypothesis is a structured claim about an extraction unit that is not trusted enough for automatic continuation. Typical hypothesis types are an uncertain entity, an uncertain triple, or an uncertain relation type. For example, from the sentence ``We present Minimum Bayes-Risk (MBR) decoding for statistical machine translation,'' the system may first propose the candidate triple (\texttt{Minimum Bayes-Risk}, \texttt{Used-for}, \texttt{statistical machine translation}). That candidate is plausible, but the subject span is not yet stable enough for admission because the text refers more precisely to \texttt{Minimum Bayes-Risk decoding}. Rather than storing the weakly normalized triple or discarding it outright, \system{} keeps it in a hypothesis state so that evidence linking and governance review can refine it before admission. This intermediate state is necessary because many low-confidence cases are not incorrect; they are only locally ambiguous, and resolving them immediately preserves document context without polluting the graph with unstable structure.

Each hypothesis includes the proposed content, the posting agent, the initial confidence, the supporting evidence, and the current pipeline context. The blackboard is a shared forum inside the shared memory system. It is not a chat interface; it is a structured coordination object. An agent posts a hypothesis to the blackboard, and the deliberation coordinator asks a set of evaluator agents to vote on it.

Each voter is not necessarily a topical expert, but an expert on a criterion. The \texttt{EntityExtractor} evaluates whether the entity hypothesis is plausible. The \texttt{RelationExtractor} evaluates whether the relation structure is coherent. The \texttt{EvidenceLinker} evaluates whether the text supports the proposal. This means the value of a vote comes from specialized perspective, not topical ownership. Deliberation here is about extraction reliability. Domain governance happens later, when the triple enters the governed KG.

Each voting agent returns a vote with rationale. The coordinator aggregates the votes and resolves the hypothesis into a keep or no-keep decision. If votes conflict, the coordinator can trigger a short structured debate. The final decision and rationale are written back to shared memory, creating an audit trail that is used both operationally and analytically. Operationally, the system reuses these records as domain-local memory: prior approvals, rejections, and revisions help later reviewers judge whether a new candidate is consistent with previously admitted patterns or should be revised. Analytically, the same trace makes it possible to inspect why a fact was admitted, rejected, or modified, which supports ablation, error analysis, and human validation.

\section{Shared Memory}

\system{} uses shared memory to coordinate extraction within a document and to maintain consistency across documents. These functions are separated because the system must handle both short-lived local uncertainty and longer-lived accumulated knowledge. During extraction, agents need access to transient state such as pending hypotheses, intermediate votes, and active evidence spans. Across documents, the system needs persistent state such as admitted entities, aliases, domain ownership patterns, and prior governance decisions.

In practice, the memory system has four regions. \textbf{Working memory} stores the current pipeline state for the active document, including candidate entities, candidate triples, pending hypotheses, and vote requests. \textbf{Semantic memory} stores accepted graph structure and canonicalized entities. Once a candidate is revised and admitted, the canonical entity, its aliases, and the admitted triple are written to semantic memory for reuse in later extraction, governance, and QA. \textbf{Procedural memory} stores reusable review patterns derived from prior decisions, such as relation signatures that were previously accepted, rejected, or revised. \textbf{Episodic memory} stores per-document processing traces, such as what was proposed, revised, or rejected in a specific document.

These memory types are used at different timescales. Working memory is short-lived and is reused continuously during a single document pass. Semantic and procedural memory persist across documents and are consulted repeatedly during governance and downstream QA. Episodic memory is consulted less frequently, mainly when the system or a human needs to inspect how a prior decision was made. This separation is important because \system{} is not only extracting facts; it is building a governed memory that must distinguish temporary uncertainty from persistent knowledge.

\section{Applications Built on Top of the Governed KG}

The governed KG is the core object in \system{}, and downstream applications operate over this governed memory rather than over raw extracted text alone.

One application is question answering. A QA orchestrator first reads the organizational structure of the graph, including the domain summary and ownership layout, and uses that structure to route the question to the most relevant domain experts. Each domain expert answers from its local subgraph, grounded in the entities, relations, and nearby evidence it owns. A synthesis stage then combines these domain-local responses into a final answer. In this way, QA is not treated as flat retrieval over the entire graph, but as a governed routing problem over domain-owned knowledge.

A second application is incremental enrichment and self-organization. As new documents arrive, candidate entities and triples are extracted and compared against the existing graph. Rather than inserting them directly, the system routes them through the same governance process used during initial construction: ownership is assigned, evidence is checked, prior domain memory is consulted, and the candidate is approved, revised, or rejected before admission. This allows the graph to grow over time while reorganizing itself around new evidence, new entities, and new cross-domain links. In that sense, the graph is not only incrementally enriched; it is also self-organizing under governed update.

\section{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate \system{} along four empirical axes.

First, we evaluate KG construction quality on the SciERC test split in fixed-schema mode. SciERC provides gold entity spans, entity types, and typed relations over scientific abstracts. In this setting, the extraction schema is fixed to the SciERC label set so that all systems are evaluated against the same target ontology. This gives hard benchmark metrics: entity F1, strict triple F1, fuzzy/mapped triple F1, and triple hallucination. Strict triple F1 requires exact agreement on subject, relation, and object. Fuzzy/mapped triple F1 is more forgiving about surface normalization and relation-label canonicalization.

Second, we run governance ablations over the same 50-document GPT-5 candidate proposal setting. These ablations ask whether \system{} is better because of governance itself or merely because some other part of the pipeline filters triples.

Third, we prepare a human triple review study. This is necessary because SciERC is a closed benchmark annotation, not an exhaustive list of every valid scientific fact in an abstract. A triple can be supported by the source text and useful for the graph while still being counted as a false positive under SciERC if it is absent from the gold annotation.

Fourth, we evaluate downstream QA on MuSiQue, a multi-hop QA dataset. MuSiQue is useful here because it tests whether the graph can support chaining across documents rather than only retrieving one local sentence. Unlike the SciERC extraction benchmark, this setting does not provide a fixed target ontology, so \system{} is run in an open-world mode in which entity and relation structure is induced from the corpus rather than constrained to a predefined schema. We run a 50-question MuSiQue slice with supporting-only contexts and compare domain-routed QA against official Microsoft GraphRAG.

\section{Results}

The completed results support four claims. Governance improves the admitted graph relative to flat insertion. The improvement is not explained by source filtering or deterministic domain rules alone. Domain experts become strongest when they carry domain-local memory. The governed graph is useful for downstream QA relative to another graph-based system.

\subsection{KG Construction Quality}

The first result compares direct flat KG insertion against two governed variants of \system{} on the same 50 SciERC test documents using GPT-5. All three systems share the same extractor, model, fixed SciERC schema, and document set. The only difference is how candidate triples are admitted into the stored graph.

The \textbf{Flat KG} baseline directly inserts extracted triples into a single global graph. It has no ownership layer, no governance decision, no revision step, and no domain-local memory. \textbf{\system{} basic governance} routes each candidate triple to the owning domain and applies governed admission before commit. The reviewer sees the candidate triple, its source evidence, the ownership assignment derived from the subject and object, and the endpoint neighborhood in the current graph. The reviewer can approve, reject, or revise the triple. \textbf{\system{} domain-memory governance} is the full system. It keeps the same governed routing and evidence-based admission, but the domain expert also carries local memory: previously accepted and rejected relation patterns, recent revised examples, endpoint membership in the owning domain, and nearby admitted triples in the domain subgraph.

The flat KG has the highest raw triple count, but most of those triples do not match the SciERC gold graph. It produces 1366 triples, of which 1266 are counted as false positives under strict SciERC matching. \system{} basic governance is substantially more selective: it reduces the admitted graph to 750 triples, improves strict triple F1 from 0.116 to 0.144, and improves fuzzy/mapped triple F1 from 0.204 to 0.305. In other words, adding domain-local memory on top of governed routing gives another clear lift over basic governance.

The absolute F1 scores are still modest because SciERC is a strict closed-label benchmark and does not treat all source-supported extracted facts as true positives. For that reason, the paper should not claim that extraction is solved. Given the same scientific extraction setting, governed admission produces a cleaner admitted graph than flat insertion, and domain-memory governance is the strongest admission policy tested. Basic governance is worth keeping in the table because it makes the mechanism legible: routing plus review already helps, and routing plus domain-local memory helps more.

\subsection{Governance Ablation}

The purpose of this experiment is to isolate the value of governance itself. All conditions are evaluated on the same 50-document GPT-5 SciERC setting, with the same extractor, document set, and schema. The main ablation rows compare different admission policies over the extracted candidate facts, while the evidence-only row provides a control that tests whether source grounding and verification alone are sufficient to explain the gains. The experiment therefore asks a focused question: once candidate triples have been extracted, what is the best way to decide which triples enter memory?

We compare four conditions. The \textbf{Flat KG} baseline has no governance at all: extracted triples are inserted directly into a single global graph. The \textbf{EvidenceLinker + Verification only} condition attaches source evidence and applies verification, but does not use domain ownership, governance routing, or reviewer-based admission. The \textbf{Global reviewer} baseline applies a single centralized LLM reviewer to the candidate triples. That reviewer only decides whether to approve or reject each triple based on the triple and its evidence. It has no notion of domain ownership, no domain-local memory, and no subgraph-specific context. Finally, \textbf{\system{}} applies the full governed admission protocol. Each candidate triple is routed to the owning domain, reviewed by the responsible domain expert, and evaluated using not only the source evidence but also domain-local memory: endpoint membership, previously accepted and rejected relation patterns, and nearby admitted triples in the domain subgraph.

The results show that not all filtering mechanisms are equally useful. The evidence-only condition performs poorly: although it attaches provenance and applies verification, it produces 1353 triples with strict triple F1 of only 0.056 and fuzzy/mapped triple F1 of 0.198. This shows that source grounding alone does not explain \system{}'s gains. By contrast, moving from flat insertion to a single global reviewer improves the admitted graph substantially: strict F1 rises from 0.116 to 0.146, and fuzzy/mapped F1 rises from 0.204 to 0.304. This confirms that some form of review is better than no review.

However, \system{} performs best overall. Compared with the global reviewer, it further improves strict F1 from 0.146 to 0.173 and fuzzy/mapped F1 from 0.304 to 0.333. The interpretation is important. The global reviewer is already a strong prompted filter, and the evidence-only condition already performs source grounding. What \system{} adds is structured governance: ownership, routing, local memory, and subgraph-aware review. Domain experts do not review triples in isolation. They evaluate them relative to what their domain already contains and what relation patterns have previously been accepted or rejected. That additional organizational context is what pushes \system{} past both simple source filtering and a single global reviewer.

\subsection{Human Triple Review}

The automatic SciERC evaluation is useful but incomplete. It penalizes any predicted triple absent from the gold annotations, even if the source text supports it. To address this, we create a human review sample from the 50-document GPT-5 construction experiment. The review set contains governed-only triples, flat-only triples, shared triples, and revised triples. Annotators judge support, relation correctness, endpoint correctness, graph usefulness, and governance decision quality.

\subsection{Downstream QA on MuSiQue}

To evaluate whether the governed graph is useful for downstream applications, we test it on MuSiQue, a multi-hop question answering benchmark designed so that answering a question requires combining facts across multiple pieces of evidence rather than retrieving a single local span. In contrast to the SciERC experiments, this setting is open-world: MuSiQue does not impose a fixed benchmark schema, so \system{} must organize entities, relations, and domains directly from unconstrained text.

We use a 50-question slice from MuSiQue 2-hop. In this setting, each question requires linking two supporting facts. For our experiment, the supporting-only corpus contains 100 contexts drawn from 99 unique titles. From this corpus, \system{} first constructs a governed graph in open-world mode by extracting entities and relations from the passages, organizing them into domain-owned subgraphs, and admitting facts through its governance pipeline. We then build a QA system on top of this graph: each question is decomposed and routed to the most relevant domain experts, those experts answer from their local subgraphs and neighboring evidence, and a final synthesis step combines their responses into a single answer. We compare this domain-routed QA system against official Microsoft GraphRAG.

Questions are routed through domain-owned subgraphs, domain experts return subgraph-grounded answers, and the final response is synthesized from these domain-local views.

\section{Conclusion}

Flat insertion produces a larger graph, but it admits many noisy triples. \system{} improves the admitted graph by adding governance, and the best result appears when domain experts carry local memory rather than acting as generic reviewers. The ablation shows that source filtering alone and a centralized global reviewer do not fully explain the result. The full domain-memory governance condition is the strongest construction system tested: it has the best strict F1, best fuzzy/mapped F1, and the cleanest admitted graph. Downstream, \system{} outperforms GraphRAG on MuSiQue graph-based QA, showing that the governed graph can serve as a useful application substrate rather than only a passive storage artifact.
